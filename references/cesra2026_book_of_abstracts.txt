Solar radio bursts observations from Solar Orbiter — Antonio Vecchio
Vecchio, A., Maksimovic, M., Bonnin, X., Pesini, A., Kretzschmar, M.,Thepthong, P.

Radboud University and Lira- Observatoire de Paris

The Radio and Plasma Waves (RPW) instrument on the ESA Solar Orbiter mission is designed to measure
electric and magnetic fields, plasma wave spectra and polarization properties, the spacecraft floating
potential, and solar radio emissions in the interplanetary medium. In particular, RPW observes solar radio
emissions up to 16 MHz, as well as associated Langmuir waves near the local plasma frequency.
In the radio domain sampled by RPW, the most commonly observed features are solar Type III bursts. The high
time and spectral resolution of RPW, combined with its unique capability to measure magnetic fluctuations up
to ~1 MHz with high sensitivity, enable unprecedented sampling of Type III radio bursts from space, allowing,
for the first time, the measurement of their magnetic component.
In this contribution, I review the most relevant results obtained with RPW in the analysis of solar Type III
bursts, including:
•      the first direct measurements of the magnetic component of solar radio emissions,
•      high temporal resolution observations of Type III radio bursts in the 3-13 MHz frequency range,
•      the first catalogue of interplanetary Type III radio bursts compiled through a citizen science campaign.

The Role of Particle Scattering Processes in Solar Flares — Gordon Emslie
Emslie, A.G.

WKU

Emissions in radio and hard X-rays are prompt diagnostics of the high energy electrons that are accelerated in
flares, which in turn provide crucial information on the primary energy release mechanism(s). For spatially
resolved radiation that is optically thin, the properties of the radiation field (intensity, photon
energy/wavelength/frequency, direction, and polarization), combined with the appropriate “cross-section” for
the emissions being observed, provide information on the local electron phase-space distribution function.
Spatially-resolved measurements can thus be used to test various electron transport models, while for
spatially unresolved measurements, an appropriate electron transport model is required in order to relate the
observed, target-averaged, properties of the electron population to those of the all-important accelerated
population.

Models of electron transport range from the early cold-target, single particle, analytic approaches to full
Fokker-Planck treatments that include physical processes such as Coulomb collisions, return current Ohmic
effects, and scattering in the target. Scattering processes include both inelastic (e.g., collisional, ohmic) and
elastic (e.g., magnetic, turbulent) scattering. We will review the evolution of scattering treatments over the
years, and we will emphasize that even if the electron energy loss rate can be adequately modeled as a cold
thick-target, with negligible diffusion in particle energy, the trajectory of the electrons, and so the spatial
distribution of the electron spectrum, still fundamentally depends on diffusive angular scattering processes.
This has substantial implications for the electron distribution function and its associated heating profile, and
hence for the atmospheric response to flare energy input. Further, we will resolve the paradox that while in a
converging magnetic field geometry magnetic scattering causes both positively- and negatively-charged
particles to experience a magnetic mirror force toward the region of weaker field, there is no associated field-
aligned body force that should be added to the MHD equations.

Simulations of plasma emission in plasmas with density fluctuations — KRAFFT Catherine
Krafft, C., Polanco-Rodriguez F.J., Volokitin A.S., and Savoini P.

Paris Saclay University

Plasma emission in the solar wind is profoundly influenced by random density fluctuations, which
fundamentally alter the dynamics of electrostatic wave turbulence excited by electron beams during type III
solar radio bursts. As these waves propagate through inhomogeneous plasma, they undergo a suite of linear
transformations—including reflection, refraction, tunneling, trapping, and mode conversion into
electromagnetic radiation. These interactions critically reshape the pathways to electromagnetic emission at
the plasma frequency. Strikingly, the canonical nonlinear three-wave decay process, wherein a Langmuir wave
decays into an electromagnetic wave and an ion acoustic wave—a mechanism long considered central to
plasma emission—becomes subordinate in the presence of strong density turbulence. Instead, linear mode
conversion at constant frequency emerges as the dominant process. This presentation advances our
understanding of solar wind plasma emission by elucidating the pivotal role of density fluctuations in
governing wave dynamics and radiation mechanisms.

K-band solar monitoring with INAF single-dish facilities: from SUNPIT to Deep Learning applications —
Marco Marongiu
Marongiu M., Pellizzoni A., Zucca P., Mancini M., Righini S., Mulas S., Iacolina M.N.

INAF - Cagliari Astronomical Observatory

The solar radio emission in the K-band range (18–26 GHz) is a fundamental workbench for studying the solar
chromosphere and corona. While the signal is dominated by the quiet-Sun free-free emission in local thermal
equilibrium, dynamic structures - such as Active Regions, Coronal Holes, and Polar Brightenings - reveal
complex interactions between solar plasma and local magnetic fields through additional gyro-magnetic
components. Since 2018, the SunDish project has mapped these phenomena, generating a unique dataset of
approximately 600 radio maps.

To better manage the scientific exploitation of the data, we developed SUNPIT (SUNdish PIpeline Tool). This
tool was specifically designed to streamline the transition from raw data to high-level scientific products.
SUNPIT optimises imaging, flux calibration, and the extraction of physical parameters, including brightness
temperatures, spectral indices, and fractional polarisation. A key advancement is the integration of Deep
Learning techniques, specifically Convolutional Autoencoders, to improve the identification and tracking of
dynamic solar features.

In this talk, we demonstrate how this synergy between Deep Learning and high-frequency radio observations
is essential for advancing our physical understanding of the Sun. This reliable and automated framework
enhances the accuracy and efficiency of key investigations, including the analysis of the solar size and the solar
atmosphere in terms of plasma density and temperature, the characterisation of gyro-resonance components
in active regions, and the analysis of coronal holes. These tools provide the robust diagnostic framework
necessary to fully exploit the INAF single-dish network for long-term solar monitoring.

Escape of energetic particles from the solar corona into the heliosphere — Mykola Gordovskyy
Gordovskyy, M., Bate, W., Brun, A.S., Sieyra, M.V., Strugarek, A. and Browning, P.K.

University of Hertfordshire

High energy ions and electrons play a crucial role in the fast energy transport in solar flares. A fraction of the
particles accelerated in flares precipitate in the solar atmosphere, heating the corona and chromosphere, and
producing non-thermal gamma-, X-ray and radio emissions. Others escape from the corona into the
heliosphere. The escaping energetic particles are an important component of the space weather. Recent
advances in computational and observational studies of solar energetic particles, have significantly improved
our understanding of how particles are transported in the solar corona, and from the corona into the
heliosphere, particularly thanks to the unprecedented data from Solar Orbiter and Parker Solar Probe
missions. In this talk, I will review the current progress in studies of solar particle acceleration and escape into
the heliosphere, with an emphasis on data-constrained computational modelling. I will present our latest
results of magnetic reconnection and particle acceleration modelling in individual solar flares, focusing, in
particular, on the effect of magnetic configuration and the location of the acceleration site on the properties
of escaping energetic particles.

Bursty acceleration and 3D trajectories of electrons in a solar flare — Shilpi Bhunia
Hayes,A.L., Klein, L., Vilmer, N., Maloney, A. S., Gallagher, T. P.

Observatoire de Paris

Solar flares are among the most energetic phenomena in the heliosphere, emitting radiation across the
electromagnetic spectrum. During flare, electrons are accelerated to non-thermal energies as a result of
magnetic reconnection. These electrons then propagate upwards and downwards from the energy release site
along magnetic field lines and produce radio and hard X-ray (HXR) emissions, respectively. Frequently, we
observe the simultaneous occurrence of multiple HXR peaks and successive radio bursts, providing compelling
evidence that these emissions originate from a common population of accelerated electrons and that the
underlying energy-release process consists of multiple local acceleration process. We present a detailed
analysis of a highly energetic flare observed by the Spectrometer Telescope for Imaging X-rays (STIX) onboard
Solar Orbiter, in conjunction with coordinated radio observations from ground- and space-based instruments
from the low corona to interplanetary space. By investigating the evolution of X-ray, and radio sources, we
find that the electrons producing HXR and radio emission have similar acceleration origins. Importantly, our
study provides observational evidence that the flare acceleration process is temporally and spatially
fragmentary, and during each of these small-scale processes, the electron beams are injected into a very
fibrous corona and produce complex HXR and radio emission

Radio Observations of Solar Active Regions with EOVSA — Sijie Yu
Sijie Yu

New Jersey Institute of Technology

Solar active regions are important radio sources that provide diagnostics of coronal plasma and magnetic
fields. This talk will review active-region radio observations with an emphasis on recent results from the
Expanded Owens Valley Solar Array (EOVSA). I will briefly describe the current EOVSA imaging spectroscopy
pipeline and its capabilities for routine multi-frequency studies. I will then discuss the main emission
mechanisms in active regions, including gyroresonance, gyrosynchrotron, and free-free emission, and highlight
key applications such as coronal magnetic field measurements.

ALMA Observations of Solar Flares — Stephen White
White, S.

AFRL

Solar flares have not been a primary target for ALMA partly due to uncertainty about the response to bright
flare emission at millimeter wavelengths. However, in 2024 May during the first ALMA observations where
circular polarization measurements of the Sun were permitted, several flares were successfully observed at
arcsecond resolution, including an X flare. Since the quiet Sun flux in the ALMA primary beam at 100 GHz is
around 60 sfu, fluxes of that order or less can readily be handled by ALMA. On behalf of the team involved,
this paper will discuss the observations in the context of models of flare heating and the response of the
chromosphere to electron beams, and directions where ALMA can help with flare studies.

First imaging observations of the evolution of Coronal Mass Ejections and Eruptive Prominences at K-band
(18-26 GHz) — Alberto Pellizzoni
Pellizzoni A., Amaro A., Napolitano D., Mulas S., Marongiu M., Egron E., Righini S., Sirigu M. on behalf of the
SunDish Team

INAF - Italy

The radio emergence of coronal mass ejections (CMEs) and eruptive prominences (EPs) has been the subject
of several studies, given their relevant role in exploring the solar atmosphere and modelling its magnetic
restructuring processes. This modelling is key to understanding the energy release mechanisms of active
regions (ARs), which are among the main drivers of space weather.
In this work, we present and analyse an unusual detection of an EP/CME event at 18.8 and 24.7 GHz
performed by the Sardinia Radio Telescope (SRT) on August 19 and 20, 2025, under the SunDish project
(https://sites.google.com/inaf.it/sundish).
We discuss the complex structure of the erupting event, including an unprecedented radio feature extending
over 15 arcmin from the solar disk and emerging from a region characterised by reconnection threads
involving helical kink instabilities, ARs, and filaments. Through spectral and polarimetric information, we can
constrain EP/CME physical parameters, including an extrapolation of the magnetic field associated with
gyrosynchrotron and synchrotron emission components.
This work paves the way for the systematic search for CME events in high-frequency radio images and their
correlation with standard coronographic images and space-based measurements.

Transient brightenings around a stable sunspot detected by ALMA at 3 mm — Alexander Nindos
Nindos, A., White, S.M., Alissandrakis, C.E., Barta, M., Bastian T.S., Berlicki, A., Brajsa, R., Chen, B., De Pontieu,
B., Eklund, H., Fleishman, G.D., Gary, D.E., Hudson, H.H., Iwai, K., Kobelski, A., Krucker, S., Loukicheva, M.,
Reardon, K., Sanchez-Maes, S., Shimojo, M., da Silva Santos, J., Skokic, I., Solanki, S., Szydlarski, M.,
Wedemeyer, S.

University of Ioannina, Greece

Using Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) observations, we performed the first systematic
survey for weak small-scale transient brightenings around a stable sunspot at 3 mm. Our dataset included
images of an 80" x 80" field of view obtained with angular resolution of a few arcsec at a cadence of 2 s. The
transient brightenings were detected as weak enhancements above the average intensity. Many of them were
also registered in Atmospheric Imaging Assembly (AIA) images obtained at several EUV channels. All detected
events were thermal in nature and the strongest of them showed loop-like or jet morphologies. Their
properties were checked against model calculations and MHD simulations of jets.

Using CoSEE-Cat to investigate the relation of solar energetic electron events with X-ray flares and type III
radio bursts — Alexander Warmuth
Warmuth, A.

Leibniz Institute for Astrophysics Potsdam (AIP)

We have developed the Comprehensive Solar Energetic Electron event Catalogue (CoSEE-Cat), a new multi-
instrument resource combining in situ and remote-sensing observations from Solar Orbiter to systematically
characterise solar energetic electron (SEE) events and their solar origins. The first data release covers 303 SEE
events detected between November 2020 and December 2022 at heliocentric distances ranging from 0.3 to
1.0 au, compiled through a joint effort of eight instrument teams (EPD, STIX, EUI, RPW, Metis, SoloHI, SWA,
and MAG). Statistical analysis of this first data release reveals a clear dichotomy between impulsive and
gradual SEE events, supporting flares and CMEs as the respective sources for these two populations. We
further show that commonly reported delays between electron solar release times and associated X-ray and
type III radio burst signatures grow systematically with heliocentric distance, indicating that interplanetary
propagation effects rather than actually delayed injections play a significant role.

Location and properties of fine frequency structures in the spectra of solar type IV radio bursts — Aneesh
Nadiger
Nadiger A, Morosan D E

University of Turku

ABSTRACT

Context : The Type IV continuum spectra is dynamic and many short-lived fine structures have been observed.
Several observations of stripes of emission and absorption bands forming zebra-like or fiber-like structures
against the background of a continuous radio spectra have been reported.
Aim : In this study, we investigate zebra patterns. Our analysis focuses on identifying the sources of the Zebra
patterns and determining their properties.
Methods : We study the Zebra bursts observed on 28th March, 2022. We used spectroscopic data from the e-
Callisto network to identify the Zebra patterns and their characteristics, radio imaging from the Nan¸cay
Radioheliograph to locate the emission sources, and ultraviolet imaging from AIA to provide
coronal imaging.
Results : We observe two distinct regions of Zebra patterns in our study. The sources are found to be
stationary, with a period of about 6 seconds and drift rates of 7–9 MHz s-1 . The instantaneous separation
between the stripes is about 4 MHz. Comparison with previous observations shows that ∆f typically
increases with increasing frequency.
Conclusions : The confined location of the Zebra sources would imply to the
sources are trapped , possibly in a coronal loop. We observe 6-8 second periods
for the zebras, indicating that the trapped plasma undergoes fast sausage mode
oscillations

Solar Radio Type V Burst: Scattering, Trapping, and Emission Process of a Dense Electron Beam — Arnold O.
Benz
Benz, A.O., Huber,C., Timmel, V., Monstein, C.

FHNW Windisch and ETH Zurich

Solar type V radio bursts are associated with type III bursts. Several processes have been proposed to interpret
the association, the electron velocity distribution, and the emission. We present the observation of a unique
type V event observed by e-CALLISTO on 2121/05/07. The type V radio emission follows a group of U bursts.
Its polarisation is opposite to the type III burst and its front edge has a U-shape. The observations conform to a
scenario where a dense electron beam first saturates its two-stream instability (causing type III emission) and
then becomes unstable to another instability, such as the electron firehose instability. This second instability
scatters some beam electrons into velocities perpendicular to the magnetic field and produces a trapped
distribution prone to electron cyclotron masering (ECM). A reduction in beaming is predicted for electron
beams continuing to interplanetary space, observable for Parker Probe and Solar Orbiter.

Dynamics of type II solar radio burst sources and spectral bridge formation — Artem Koval
Koval, A., Karlický, M., Bárta, M.

Astronomical Institute of the Czech Academy of Sciences

Type II solar radio bursts are commonly associated with shock waves propagating through the solar corona in
connection with coronal mass ejections. A characteristic feature of these bursts is band splitting, where the
emission lane divides into two closely spaced, nearly parallel bands. While its origin is still debated, it is often
interpreted in terms of either plasma emission from different shock regions (upstream and downstream areas)
or spatially separated acceleration sites along the shock front. We analyze LOFAR observations of a type II
radio burst that display fine spectral structures connecting the split bands, referred to here as spectral bridges.
These features exhibit both positive and negative frequency drifts. Their properties suggest that they are not
random appearances, but are instead linked to evolving physical conditions within the shock or/and the
coronal environments. In this study, we explore a scenario of the formation of spectral bridges. In addition, we
examine the motion of the emission sources associated with the split bands and discuss how their dynamics
may reflect the underlying structure and evolution of the shock.

The effect of solar wind turbulence on the clumpiness and local generation of Langmuir waves — Camille
Yasmina Lorfing
Lorfing, C.Y., Reid, H., Pisa, D., Nicolaou, G., Trotta, D., Krupar, V., Magdalenic, J.

KU Leuven

How do solar wind turbulence affect the clumpiness and local
generation of Langmuir waves? Solar electrons beams are accelerated in the corona to then travel out into the
solar wind. These beams of non-thermal electrons evolve as a function of distance from the Sun, interacting
with the turbulent background plasma and growing Langmuir waves as they propagate. Subsequent radio
emission is also seen in the form of type III bursts. Where solar wind turbulence comes from and how
turbulent energy is dissipated and evolves within the heliosphere has been a long lasting science question,
studied through numerous simulations and observations since the 1970s. However, previous studies looking at
the role of turbulence in the clumpiness and generation of Langmuir waves have never done so at such a high
temporal resolution as offered by Solar Orbiter. Through a combination of in-situ and remote sensing
observations of plasma conditions at the spacecraft and associated radio emission, we study the influence of
density fluctuations in the solar wind on the local generation of plasma waves between 0.3 and 1 AU. With 35
events between November 2020 and January 2025, we produce a statistical study of the role of turbulence on
wave-particle interactions with heliocentric distance, and different latitudes outside of the ecliptic plane. This
allows us to determine a relationship between density fluctuations, solar wind speed, heliocentric distance,
and the clumpiness and waveform of Langmuir waves. We also study the associated radio emission to
indentify a link between the intensity and maximum frequency of this radio emission and the quantity of
Langmuir waves growth above thermal level at the spacecraft. Understanding the mechanisms driving
Langmuir waves growth in different solar wind conditions will permit more accurate predictions of wave-
particle interactions and propagation in the heliosphere. Moreover, our work not only supports previous
theoretical work but can be complemented by measurements from the Parker Solar Probe spacecraft.

Evolution of coronal radio wave scattering during an M class solar flare — Christian Vocks
Vocks, C., Bisi, M., Dabrowski, B., Morosan, D., Gallagher, P., Krankowski, A., Magdalenic, J., Mann, G.,
Marque, C., Matyjasiak, B., Rothkaehl, H., Zucca, P.

Leibniz-Institut für Astrophysik Potsdam (AIP)

We present LOFAR observations of an M class flare with intense type III radio bursts. Some isolated burst have
a fundamental-harmonic structure, but for most bursts this is not visible due to a rapid succession of bursts.
Spectroscopic imaging with LOFAR shows type III bursts as compact sources for a given frequency. The
intensity varies with burst evolution, and transient dual-source structures appear. We interpret these as
signatures of fundamental and harmonic emission, the latter from a higher location in the corona.
Fundamental-harmonic pairs, e.g. fundamental emission at 35 MHz and harmonic emission at 70 MHz, should
originate from the same plasma volume. Differences in their positions and intensity variations are expected
since radio wave transport effects in the corona, like scattering and refraction, affect fundamental more than
harmonic emission. We show the evolution of position differences of fundamental-harmonic pairs during

Plasma flow in the solar atmosphere along magnetic fields driven by the Kelvin force — Kiyoto Shibasaki
Shibasaki, L.

Solar Physics Research Inc.

The solar atmosphere is in a plasma state. Magnetic field lines are frozen into the plasma; they move together
perpendicular to the field. Along the magnetic field, however, the primary forces governing dynamics are the
gas pressure gradient force and gravity. Consequently, in a steady state, hydrostatic equilibrium is achieved.
However, observations reveal temperature-dependent upward/downward flows along the magnetic field. In
this work, we propose a mechanism to explain flows along the field. Charged particles exhibit gyro motion in a
magnetic field due to the Lorentz force, thereby possessing a magnetic moment. However, statistical
thermodynamics states that magnetism does not arise in classical physics (Bohr-van Leeuwen Theorem). This
theorem can be proven incorrect. Magnetism does arise in classical physics, and plasma possesses a magnetic
moment. The Kelvin force acts on the magnetic moment, with a component parallel to the magnetic field.
Since plasma is diamagnetic, it experiences a force directed toward regions of weaker magnetic field strength.
In the solar atmosphere, the magnetic field is weaker upward, so the magnetic Kelvin force points upward.
Moreover, this magnetic moment is proportional to the plasma temperature. When this upward Kelvin force
exceeds the downward gravity force, the plasma flows upward. This explains the temperature-dependent
upward/downward flows. Introducing the magnetic moment in this way allows us to understand the dynamics
of plasma along the magnetic field in the solar atmosphere.

the underlying emission mechanisms and local plasma conditions. Plasma emission theory predicts these

recently, by the detection of linearly polarized emission also from a type II burst using the MWA. These
findings also have broader implications for understanding the emission mechanism of stellar radio bursts,
where linearly polarized component are often observed and key insights are drawn from our understanding of
analogous solar phenomena. In this talk, I will present the detection and characteristics of linear polarization
components of metric solar emissions, focusing on a type II burst, and briefly discuss possible generation
mechanisms for the observed linear polarization.
